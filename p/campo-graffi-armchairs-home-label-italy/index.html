Turin, 1950s. The city that gave the world Fiat, Lavazza, and some of the most daring furniture of the twentieth century. These armchairs are attributed to Franco Campo and Carlo Graffi, the Turin architects who studied under Carlo Mollino. The same model has been offered several times at PIASA as attributed to the duo, in most cases with the Turin workshop Apelli & Varesio named as maker.

The influence of their teacher Carlo Mollino is unmistakable. Note the angular, skeletal side frames, the long slotted cut-outs in the legs and the moulded seat rail that seems to float between them. This was the Turinese school at its most ambitious: furniture as sculpture, function dissolved into art. The solid wood structure is complex, warm, and alive with the kind of craftsmanship that demanded both artistic vision and technical mastery.

The chairs have recently been reupholstered in the Netherlands in a dark brown cotton velvet, which provides a striking contrast to the intricate wooden framework and complements the sculptural warmth of the wood. The overall condition is good, with only minor signs of use consistent with their age.

The chairs are not signed, which was not unusual at the time. The attribution rests on the design itself and on its appearances at auction, where the model has been catalogued as attributed to Campo and Graffi. According to the auction house Wright, the work of the duo became more streamlined and sculptural in the later 1950s, with an emphasis on the joints.

This model comes from an explosive period of Italian design history. In a contemporary interior, these chairs bring not just beauty and comfort, but a direct connection to one of the most creatively charged moments in twentieth-century furniture.
