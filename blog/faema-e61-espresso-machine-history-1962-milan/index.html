Faema E61: The 1962 Machine That Invented Espresso

By Marco van den Bergh · July 27, 2026

Its name comes from an eclipse. On 15 February 1961, a total solar eclipse crossed Italy, and Faema — Fabbrica Apparecchiature Elettromeccaniche e Affini, a coffee machine maker in Milan — chose that moment to launch the machine that would carry its date into its name: the E61. It was a fitting christening. Like the eclipse itself, the E61 blotted out everything that came before it in the world of espresso.

Until 1961, every serious espresso machine relied on a manual lever — a barista pulling a spring-loaded piston by hand, pressure rising and falling with the strength of their arm. The E61 replaced that gesture with a motorised volumetric pump, delivering fixed, consistent water pressure at the flick of a switch. It sounds like a small mechanical substitution. In practice it reinvented the category: for the first time, a single barista working alone could pull shot after shot with the same pressure, the same crema, the same result. Within a decade it was Italy's best-selling coffee machine, and the "E61 group head" it introduced — the polished, part-chrome brewing unit on top of the machine — became the template every high-end home and commercial espresso machine still borrows from today.

This two-group example, built in Milan in 1962, is the professional configuration made for busy bars — a machine designed to serve, not just to sit on a counter. Its chrome shell has the sculptural, unapologetic confidence of Italian industrial design at its peak, all rounded edges and mechanical detail treated like ornament. That same instinct — turning a working object into a design statement — runs through other Italian pieces of the era, from the stainless surfaces of the P.R.O.F.A. medical display cabinets from Padua to the polished curve of a Goffredo Reggiani chrome arc floor lamp.

Six decades on, this E61 still carries the presence it was built with. The chrome holds its shine, the mechanics are intact, and every lever tells the same story: this is the machine that changed how the world drinks coffee, not a replica of it.
